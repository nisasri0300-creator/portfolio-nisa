Omnichannel Store Performance & Sales Analytics

Strategic Business Framework | Pretty Girl / Omnichannel Commerce

A strategic framework for turning multiple selling channels into one measurable business system — connecting customer journeys, sales, products, inventory, operations, and profitability.

Why Omnichannel?

The reference research describes omnichannel as an integrated experience across multiple formats, rather than simply operating several independent channels. Its 2026 summary reports that 91% of consumers are omnichannel shoppers, shoppers interact with brands at an average of 11 touchpoints before buying, and omnichannel engagement is associated with higher retention and sales outcomes. These figures are used as strategic context, not as targets for the project.

Reference Signal Reported Figure Strategic Meaning
Omnichannel shoppers 91% Customers increasingly interact across multiple formats.

Omnichannel engagement +9.5% Integration can be evaluated as a business-growth lever.
Omnichannel shopper spend +16% per order Basket value becomes an important KPI.

1. Owner Vision

The objective is not simply to increase marketplace sales. The objective is to build a commerce engine where every channel contributes to acquisition, conversion, customer retention, revenue, profitability, and sustainable growth.

TRAFFIC → CONVERSION → ORDER → SALES → PROFIT → RETENTION → GROWTH

2. The Omnichannel Business Model

  • • Customer layer: understand how customers discover, compare, purchase, return, and repurchase.
  • • Channel layer: measure each marketplace or digital channel while treating the customer relationship as one ecosystem.
  • • Product layer: identify hero, traffic, profit, support, and slow-moving products.
  • • Operations layer: connect demand with inventory, replenishment, fulfillment, and production.
  • • Financial layer: separate revenue growth from profitable growth.

4. Customer Journey & Conversion

The system follows the customer journey instead of treating each marketplace as an isolated business:

IMPRESSION → PRODUCT VIEW → ADD TO CART → CHECKOUT → ORDER → REPEAT ORDER
  • • High traffic + low conversion → investigate product presentation, price, reviews, trust, and offer.
  • • High add-to-cart + low checkout → investigate shipping, voucher, pricing, and checkout friction.
  • • Strong first purchase + weak repeat purchase → investigate product satisfaction, retention, and post-purchase experience.

5. Product Strategy

Role Strategic Action
Hero Product Scale demand while protecting availability and margin.
Traffic Product Use as an entry point to attract customers.
Profit Product Promote through positioning, bundle, and cross-sell.
Support Product Increase basket size and product combinations.
Slow-Moving Product Control inventory through bundle, promotion, or clearance.

6. Inventory as a Growth Constraint

DEMAND → FORECAST → INVENTORY → REPLENISHMENT → AVAILABILITY → SALES

A successful sales strategy can create a new problem if inventory cannot support demand. The dashboard therefore needs to connect sales signals with stockout risk, excess inventory, replenishment, and production requirements.

7. Campaign & Content Strategy

  • • Acquisition — attract new customers.
  • • Conversion — turn traffic into orders.
  • • Basket Growth — increase average order value through bundles and cross-selling.
  • • Clearance — reduce slow-moving or excess inventory.
  • • Retention — encourage repeat purchases.

Content performance should be connected to product visits, conversion, and revenue rather than engagement alone.

8. Strategic Roadmap

Phase Focus Output
Phase 1 Data Foundation Unified product, SKU, channel, sales, customer, and inventory definitions.
Phase 2 Performance Visibility Owner dashboard covering business, channel, product, customer, and operations.
Phase 3 Optimization Improve conversion, product mix, campaign efficiency, inventory availability, and retention.
Phase 4 Integration Connect demand signals with replenishment, production, and inventory planning.
Phase 5 Scale Use performance evidence to allocate capital, inventory, marketing, and channel priorities.

9. Definition of Success

Success is not defined by one marketplace, one campaign, or one revenue number. The project succeeds when management can see the whole commercial system, identify the constraint behind performance, act on it, and measure the result.
